Cities around the world are rediscovering the rooftop as a place to grow food. In dense neighborhoods where ground-level land is expensive or simply unavailable, flat roofs offer thousands of square meters of unused space that receives full sunlight for most of the day. Over the past decade, a growing number of restaurants, schools and apartment cooperatives have converted these surfaces into vegetable gardens, beehives and small greenhouses.

The appeal is partly practical. Produce picked a few meters from the kitchen needs no refrigerated transport, so chefs can serve herbs and salad greens within minutes of harvest. Rooftop plantings also insulate the buildings beneath them. Studies of green roofs in temperate climates have found that a layer of soil and vegetation can reduce summer indoor temperatures noticeably and cut the energy used for cooling. The plants absorb rainwater as well, which eases pressure on storm drains during heavy downpours, a growing problem as extreme weather becomes more frequent.

There are real obstacles, however. Soil is heavy, and a saturated planting bed can weigh several hundred kilograms per square meter, so many older buildings need structural assessments before anyone carries a single bag of compost upstairs. Waterproofing must be perfect, because even a small leak can cause expensive damage to the apartments below. Wind is another challenge: plants that thrive at street level can dry out or be torn apart at the top of a tall building, which pushes growers toward hardy crops such as kale, chard and thyme.

Economics decide whether a project survives. Most rooftop farms do not earn enough from vegetables alone to cover rent and labor, so the successful ones combine several income streams. Some sell subscriptions to nearby households, others host workshops and corporate events, and a few receive city subsidies in recognition of the environmental benefits. Operators say the most reliable customers are restaurants willing to pay a premium for ingredients they can see growing from their own dining room windows.

Urban planners are beginning to write rooftop agriculture into building codes. Several cities now offer tax relief or faster permits to developers who include green roofs in new construction, and a handful require them on large commercial buildings. Advocates argue that the policy is inexpensive compared with the benefits, which include cooler streets, cleaner air, habitat for pollinators and a stronger connection between residents and the food they eat. Critics counter that rooftop farms will never feed a city, and that the money might achieve more if it were spent on improving transport links to rural farmland.

Most experts land somewhere in the middle. Rooftop farming is unlikely to replace conventional agriculture, but it can supply fresh produce to neighborhoods that lack it, teach children where food comes from, and make buildings more resilient to heat and flooding. As construction methods improve and lightweight growing media become cheaper, the number of working roof gardens is expected to keep rising, turning one of the most overlooked parts of the skyline into productive land.
